How plants learn to forget the drought

How plants learn to forget the drought

nature news · jul 29, 2026 · 4 min read

For decades, biologists assumed that a plant's memory of drought was a burden it could not afford to keep. New work from a team in Madrid suggests the opposite: forgetting, it turns out, is an active and surprisingly elegant process.

When water runs short, many plants enter a dormant state, folding their metabolic ambitions into a tight, protective knot. What remained unclear was how they decided when to unfold again — and how a seedling could stay resilient after repeated stress.

The team tracked more than four thousand seedlings across three dry seasons. They found that seeds which had experienced one mild drought were quicker to germinate the following spring, yet plants that remembered every dry spell grew more slowly, as if caution itself had a price.

The findings are not entirely reassuring. In fragile ecosystems, where rains arrive later each decade, a plant that forgets too quickly may spend its reserves on growth it cannot sustain. "There is a sweet spot," says lead author Marta Vidal, "between learning from the past and being imprisoned by it."

For farmers, the work hints at seed treatments that could tune this memory — priming crops to recall just enough hardship to survive, but not so much that they never thrive.
